# Abstract

What we built. For Track 2b, "Circuit-level interventions": one pipeline on CohereLabs/tiny-aya-global that chains three track repos. CircuitKIT finds the circuit and says which attention heads and MLPs to change. SafeTune gives the baseline to beat (refusal-direction steering). AuditKIT scores the side-effect benchmarks before and after. We added what the toolkits lack: control groups (the same change on random, lowest-scoring and all components) and a five-part "Nothing-Else-Broke" audit: general ability, safety, five languages (English, Hindi, Arabic, French, Swahili), other circuits, and errors on unedited facts. Four of the five parts were run on the adapters.

The model that came out. A LoRA adapter restricted to the top 30 % of components by refusal-circuit score. The test set is 159 held-out harmful requests per language; two of its three harm categories were never trained on, and an LLM judge reads the replies. Tiny Aya helps with a harmful request 12.4 % of the time in Hindi, Arabic, French and Swahili, and 8.8 % in English. With the adapter: 3.8 % and 1.9 % (one run, picked in advance; 5.9 % outside English averaged over three seeds). SafeTune steering reaches 8.8 % and 3.8 %, and refuses harmless prompts more often (15.3 % against 8.8 %; base model 3.1 %).

What the audit caught. This first adapter raised perplexity by 26 % on English text and, outside English, usually kept writing after it refused. Both defects likely came from our own training setup. A second adapter fixes them: perplexity is 1.02 times the base and 84-94 % of its replies to harmful requests are just the refusal, at 7.1 % and 4.4 % compliance. We release both and recommend the second. Costs that remain: refusals of harmless prompts rise 3-6 points, mostly in Swahili, and accuracy on reworded facts falls 3.5-4 points.

The honest finding. The circuit is not why it works. The 30 % set holds 33-35 of the model's 36 MLPs, and the same adapter trained on random components of the same size does as well: the difference is under one point. In 393 earlier runs with steering, LoRA and MEMIT knowledge editing, on fact correction and on refusal, the circuit never passed our pre-registered locality test.

Two findings others can use. First, the usual side-effect check is too weak. Of 92 interventions that pass "perplexity within 5 %, MMLU within 5 points", 31 had moved something we audit by 10 points or more (random noise of the same size: 2 of 68; threshold set after the study). Second, counting refusal phrases misleads outside English. Our phrase detector shows a 41.5-point refusal gap between English and Hindi; a judge that reads the replies finds 23 points, and only 2.5 points in compliance.

Released. Code in three repos, two adapters, datasets, per-item judgments and logs on Hugging Face, a Space, the pre-registration with 24 dated amendments, six toolkit defects with fixes, and a cost comparison against closed APIs.
